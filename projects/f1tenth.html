Autonomous F1Tenth Race Car

Electromechanical design, wiring architecture, and a Gaussian path planner for a competitive 1:10 scale autonomous racer that stays stable past 20 mph.

RoleVice President, Hardware
OrganizationBU F1Tenth / RoboRacer
TimelineDec 2024 – Present
FocusElectromechanical design, sensing, real-time control

Overview

I work on the development of a 1:10 scale autonomous race car as part of Boston University's F1Tenth Autonomous Car Racing Organization, a hands-on robotics project team integrating electromechanical design, sensing, and control. The goal is to design, assemble, and iterate on a fully functional autonomous vehicle capable of navigating competitive race tracks using onboard sensing and real-time control.

As Vice President of Hardware, my work centers on the electromechanical design of the platform and the physical manufacturing of its components. I also contribute on the software side, working with LIDAR and computer vision to enable dynamic path planning and real-time obstacle navigation.

More about the team at buf1tenth.com.

Electromechanical Design and Wiring Architecture

I was responsible for designing and implementing the electromechanical system and wiring architecture, selecting and integrating compute, sensing, power, and actuation components with a focus on reliability, serviceability, and real-world performance.

I designed and assembled the full wiring harness, routing power and signal lines to minimize electrical noise, reduce mechanical strain, and improve accessibility for debugging and maintenance. Connector selection, cable management, strain relief, and vibration resistance all received specific attention, since consistent performance during high-speed operation depends on them. Early in the process the main challenge was developing an electrical framework capable of sustaining the speeds the vehicle actually runs at.

Compute and perception

I selected the NVIDIA Jetson NX as the primary compute module for its ability to support real-time LIDAR processing and future vision-based autonomy while staying compact enough for a 1:10 scale vehicle. It was integrated with attention to thermal exposure, vibration isolation, and serviceability, particularly after a Jetson failure during testing made the case for robust mounting and a rapid replacement strategy.

For perception we use the Hokuyo UST-10LX 2D LIDAR, chosen for its lightweight form factor, precise scanning, and suitability for fast dynamic obstacle detection. Its placement and wiring were designed to maintain consistent scan geometry while minimizing interference from drivetrain vibration and power electronics.

Failure that changed the design

Initial testing showed that powering all subsystems from a single battery introduced electrical instability and inconsistent performance. We redesigned the power architecture to split the system across two battery sources, which significantly improved stability and reduced stress on sensitive components.

A Gens Ace 3S 11.1 V 5000 mAh LiPo was selected as the primary energy source for its current-delivery capability and endurance under sustained load, supported by a CC BEC Pro switching regulator that delivers stable voltage to the control electronics while isolating them from motor noise.

Path Planning and Real-Time Navigation

Although most of my time goes to electromechanical design and hardware integration, I also contributed to the path-planning and autonomous navigation pipeline, focused on translating LIDAR-based perception into real-time, physically executable motion commands under competitive conditions.

Our existing control logic underperformed above 20 mph. To address it we developed a new control approach using Gaussian-based direction selection. The algorithm processes LIDAR scan data and assigns directional weights across the vehicle's forward field of view. Unlike the previous approach, which selected paths based on single minimum-distance measurements, this method models free space using Gaussian distributions, favoring wider and safer openings while naturally smoothing noisy sensor data.

  • Robustness to spurious LIDAR readings
  • Smoother directional outputs, reducing abrupt steering changes
  • Improved stability at high speed, especially in narrow or cluttered track sections

The Gaussian weighting effectively transforms raw LIDAR distances into a continuous cost landscape, enabling more precise direction selection under faster racing conditions with dynamic obstacles and sharper turns.

The control layer built on the planner's output. Rather than issuing aggressive or discontinuous commands, it scales steering based on curvature and vehicle speed and adjusts throttle to maintain stability through tight turns, reducing oscillation caused by overcorrection or sensor noise. The point was to make sure planned trajectories were not just optimal in theory but physically achievable, accounting for actuator response, steering limits, and traction.
