The Deskinator

A fully autonomous desktop-cleaning robot that detects boundaries, plans coverage paths, and removes debris without human intervention, built under strict time, safety, and cost constraints.

RoleMechanical and Robotics Systems Lead
TimelineSep 2025 – Dec 2025
FocusElectromechanical design, motion systems, embedded hardware, autonomy
LinksVideo · Technical report

Overview and Motivation

This project explores how an autonomous electromechanical system can be designed to reliably perform full-surface coverage in a constrained, human-facing environment. The Deskinator was built to translate motion-planning and autonomy concepts into a physically robust, low-cost robot, emphasizing safe operation, repeatable performance, and real-world reliability.

The motivation was simple. There is a gap between theoretical autonomy concepts and physically realizable systems that operate consistently under real constraints. As a student, my desk is everything to me, from a place to do classwork to where I eat lunch, so the most applicable use of an autonomous system in my life was a desktop-cleaning robot.

Beyond demonstrating autonomy, the project emphasizes repeatability, manufacturability, and robustness. Those findings are relevant not only to service robotics but to manufacturing and product development, where consistent performance under real-world variability is the whole game.

Electromechanical Design and Wiring Architecture

I designed and manufactured every mechanical component of the Deskinator, including the chassis, bumper system, vacuum sweep system, and wheels. I also designed and implemented the electrical system, integrating motors, sensors, power, and computing hardware into a compact, serviceable layout.

I developed the full wiring architecture and integrated dual power rails, 12 V and 5 V, to separately power high-load actuators and sensitive electronics. Wiring was routed to minimize EMI and mechanical strain during motion while staying modular for debugging, replacement, and testing.

Motor Selection and Drive System

I selected NEMA 17 stepper motors paired with A4988 stepper drivers to achieve precise, repeatable motion while staying desk-safe. During development we replaced an initial DC motor and H-bridge setup after identifying thermal foldback and a loss of microstepping.

The A4988 current-limited drivers maintain a stable sinusoidal phase current under load, keeping the motors at a consistent microstepping limit. Before selecting the NEMA 17s, we ran extensive testing measuring angular error over time, prioritizing repeatability and accuracy. Many of the steppers we tested did not meet the small angular error required for safe operation.

The result was smooth, jitter-free differential drive motion with reliable odometry for autonomous navigation.

Sensor Integration and Embedded Debugging

Sensor selection mattered because our SLAM approach relies directly on front-facing sensors for accurate edge detection and obstacle avoidance. After testing a range of infrared and proximity sensors, we selected the APDS9960 as the primary edge-detection hardware. One APDS9960 also drives gesture-based touchless activation in the final design. The central compute unit was a Raspberry Pi 4B.

Custom Vacuum System

Of everything I designed for the Deskinator, the vacuum system was the hardest, and the final version worked flawlessly. A vacuum scoop mounts to the bottom of the main frame, powered by a 12 V DC fan, with a custom chute geometry directing debris into a removable container.

Result

Testing showed 99.4% debris removal while staying inside project cost and weight limits. The detachable container was key to the usability goal: minimal maintenance, no manual movement, no manual control.

Manufacturing Process

I manufactured every component. The wheels were laser-cut from stock plywood on an Epilog laser cutter and covered with rubber tread. The main body was 3D printed in PLA on an FDM printer. The hardware mounting plate was laser-cut acrylic, with components secured using M3 hex screws.

Sensor flaps, the vacuum chute and container, the battery holder, NEMA 17 motor mounts, and fan mounts were all 3D printed in PLA on a Bambu Lab FDM printer. Every component was first prototyped in cardboard and wood to evaluate effectiveness and find design improvements before final fabrication.

Localization and SLAM

While my work focused on hardware and manufacturing, I worked closely with the software side to make sure mechanical decisions supported autonomy. I contributed to the localization and SLAM pipeline, combining wheel odometry with IMU data through an Extended Kalman Filter to reduce drift and maintain stable pose estimates.

My specific responsibility was making sure the SLAM approach aligned with the robot's mechanical and sensing limitations, including wheel slip, motor resolution, and sensor noise. Through iterative testing I tuned filter parameters and motion constraints to improve localization stability, letting the robot hold accurate positioning across full cleaning cycles with no external markers or beacons.
