EMBR

A $30 chest strap that estimates your core body temperature from your heart rate, and tells you when it doesn't know.

The number that started it

Exertional heat stroke is close to 100% survivable if core temperature comes down below 40 °C within thirty minutes. The problem is knowing you're getting there. Core temperature can only really be measured with a swallowed pill or a rectal probe, so on a job site people go by feel, and by the time you feel it you are already in trouble. Roughly 19,000 workers die from heat every year.

There is a standard workaround. The US Army published ECTemp, a Kalman filter that estimates core temperature from heart rate, and commercial products are built on it. We tested it on 14,041 minutes of real heat-chamber data with rectal probes as the reference.

The finding the whole project turned on

ECTemp's own "90% confident" range contained the true temperature 34% of the time. In 34 of 73 trials where someone's real core temperature crossed 38.5 °C, its 38.5 °C alarm never fired at all.

So the project stopped being "build a temperature wearable" and became something more specific: build the honest version. A range that is actually right as often as it claims. A device that says "I can't tell right now" instead of guessing. And a hard rule that it never hides the reading from someone who is already overheating.

The early warning number is the one I care about most. By the time a person's true core temperature reached 38.5 °C, the 38.0 °C Watch alert was already on in 65 of 73 trials, a median of 12 minutes ahead, and it never failed to fire. The original filter missed it five times.

My part

I owned the wiring documentation and the enclosure.

I drew the wiring diagrams the rest of the team built from, so whoever was holding the soldering iron could replicate the circuit exactly instead of tracing it off somebody else's board. On a weekend where the hardware changed under us twice, that is the difference between one correct build and four slightly different ones.

The case I designed in Onshape, across two rapid prototyping cycles. The first was a rough PLA print, enough to find out whether the thing went together and sat on a chest at all. The second was the one that mattered: PETG, and shaped around how a person actually wears it rather than around the boards inside it. A few decisions:

  • A 150 mm radius curved back so the case bridges the breastbone instead of rocking on it.
  • Strap loops at skin level, so the strap can't tip the case off the chest when the wearer moves.
  • Two thermistors set into windows in the case floor, touching skin directly, with a third hanging outside the strap to read ambient air.
  • Vents, drains, a cable slot for the external leads, and a USB notch reachable with the lid on.

It took the usual beating from reality. The wire bundle did not fit under the lid, so the case was reprinted 12 mm taller. The screw holes had to be opened up because PETG prints them tight. Halfway through the build we lost the humidity sensor and one of the two pulse sensors, so the wiring, the firmware, the Bluetooth frame and the app all had to be reworked together around what we actually had.

The resistor I am oddly proud of

R4 does nothing except hold GPIO8 low. Without it the pin floats, a leftover battery check in the firmware reads a dying battery that does not exist, and the board puts itself to sleep mid-test. It is a ten cent part that stops the device from quietly turning itself off, which is exactly the kind of failure you only find by building the thing.

How it works

An ESP32-S3 samples the sensors at 100 Hz on a dedicated core. Heart rate comes from autocorrelation of the pulse signal, with a cadence check that rejects "pulses" that are really the sensor bouncing in step with the wearer's stride. The estimate steps once a minute, which is the rate ECTemp was published and calibrated at, and if there is no trustworthy heart rate for a minute it predicts without updating so its uncertainty honestly grows.

Three layers sit on top of each other. The published Kalman filter. Then a ridge regression correction, capped at ±1 °C, whose largest input after the filter's own estimate is heart rate above the wearer's own resting rate. In other words it learned that the thing ECTemp gets wrong is assuming one curve fits everyone. Then split conformal prediction, where held-out absolute errors are sorted into bins and the range half-width is the quantile in each bin, which guarantees the coverage without assuming anything about the shape of the errors.

Validation was nested leave-one-subject-out, so every person was scored by a model and a calibration fitted on the other 36, then tested externally on a 65-person dataset the model had never seen. The same conformal table is generated once by the Python pipeline and compiled into the firmware, so the device and the app cannot disagree with each other.

What it gets wrong

We put these on the results page rather than burying them, and I think that is a large part of why it won.

  • Coverage drops to 71% above 38.5 °C, because the estimate lags a fast rise. That is precisely the danger zone. It is also why the real early warning sits at 38.0 °C instead.
  • Catching more true cases cost specificity: sensitivity at 38.0 °C went from 0.61 to 0.75, specificity from 0.90 to 0.84.
  • On long walks in the external dataset, heart rate drifts up while core temperature levels off, so EMBR over-reads. More false alarms, not more misses, which is the direction you want to fail in.
  • Healthy adults aged 19 to 40, in lab protocols. Not a medical device, and not clinically validated.

What I took from it

Calibration matters more than accuracy. A model that is slightly worse on average but honest about its uncertainty is more useful than a confident one that is wrong a third of the time, and that is a hardware lesson as much as a statistics one.

The other one is that averages hide failures. The overall coverage number looks excellent. Slice it by the true outcome and the danger zone is the weakest band, which is the only slice that actually matters for a safety device.
