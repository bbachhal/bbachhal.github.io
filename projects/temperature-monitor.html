Room Temperature Monitoring System

An embedded system built end to end: sensing, control logic, electrical safety, manufacturable wiring, and enclosure integration.

RoleSole designer and builder
TimelineFirst-year design project
StackArduino Uno, TMP36, 16×2 I²C LCD, Onshape
LinksTechnical report

Overview and Motivation

This project explores how a simple, reliable embedded system can continuously monitor environmental conditions and provide clear real-time feedback. The motivation was to move past simulations and isolated circuits and build a fully integrated hardware system, with real attention to component selection, electrical safety, manufacturable wiring, and enclosure integration.

Personally, this was the stepping stone to everything harder that came after, including the Deskinator and the F1Tenth race car. The wiring, coding, and mechanical integration challenges here were where I learned how those problems actually behave. I still consider it one of the most important projects of my undergraduate career.

System Architecture and Embedded Control

The system is built around an Arduino Uno acting as the central controller for sensing, computation, and output logic. An analog TMP36 temperature sensor provides a voltage proportional to ambient temperature, which the Arduino reads through its ADC and converts to both Celsius and Fahrenheit in software.

Readings display on a 16×2 I²C LCD while embedded logic continuously evaluates whether the measured temperature falls outside the defined 80–90 °F safe range. When thresholds are exceeded, the Arduino activates a red LED warning indicator and a piezo buzzer.

Electrical Design and Wiring

I designed and assembled the full electrical system with a focus on clarity, reliability, and safety. Power and signal lines were organized using 22 AWG solid-core wiring, with durable connections made through heat shrink tubing, twist caps, and spade connectors. A consistent color-coding scheme simplified debugging and maintenance after preliminary testing. I integrated an on/off switch and a battery clip for standalone operation, powered by a 9 V battery within the Arduino's supported input range.

Component Selection and Electrical Calculations

All choices were guided by electrical constraints and reliability. The TMP36 was chosen for its linear voltage-to-temperature response, wide operating range, and compact size. Resistors were sized using Ohm's law to protect the LEDs: 1 kΩ for the green LED and 220 Ω for the red, ensuring every component operates within safe current and voltage limits for continuous use on a 9 V battery.

Measured result

Total system current draw measured approximately 80 mA, yielding an estimated 9.5 hours of continuous operation. This exceeded the preliminary design calculation, which showed the improvements made during the design process had worked.

Mechanical Integration and Enclosure

All hardware was housed in a 3D-printed ABS enclosure with a dedicated acrylic mounting plate for the Arduino and LCD cut on an Epilog laser cutter, internal PLA battery holders and wiring channels, and secure fastening through screws and right-angle connectors. The design provides mechanical protection, consistent component placement, and a clean assembly that survives repeated handling and testing.

All CAD was created in Onshape, chosen for accessibility at the time. I only gained access to SolidWorks and AutoCAD after finishing this project, and in retrospect either would have simplified the process and offered more functionality.

Testing, Performance, and Validation

The prototype was validated through repeated testing, demonstrating accurate real-time readings, reliable LCD updates in both units, consistent alarm activation outside the 80–90 °F range, and stable operation at a 5 V internal logic level.

The honest limitations: I wanted a more compact enclosure, but rearranging internal components would have compromised wiring organization and clarity. Environmental exposure of the TMP36 may also introduce error over long-term use. Future improvements would include a sensor more resistant to environmental conditions, or shielding to improve measurement reliability.
