Where this stands

Everything on this page is design work. The concept, the system architecture, the bill of materials and the full mechanical assembly exist; no hardware has been built yet. I am publishing it because the reasoning is the part worth showing, and because I would rather be judged on a design I can defend than on a photo of something finished.

The problem

When a building collapses, rescuers cannot see into voids, cell networks are down, and radios struggle through rubble. Thermal sensing alone has a specific blind spot: it can tell you a warm body is present, but it cannot tell you whether that person is conscious. That distinction is exactly what drives triage, so a sensor that cannot make it leaves the hardest decision unanswered.

The concept

Responders throw or place small nodes onto the debris. Each node knocks a pattern into the structure, listens through a contact sensor for taps coming back, and passively watches for breathing, body heat, phones and gas. Nodes relay short prioritised alerts over a LoRa mesh to a laptop at the perimeter.

The nodes are weighted darts, so they fly and land nose-first and the contact sensor actually meets the debris. If a radio link drops, a node holds its alerts until a responder carrying a handheld collector walks back within range.

The reason I like this idea is that it is not a new procedure. Rescue teams already knock, call out and listen. ECHO automates a routine they train on, and adds a lifeline through the victim's own phone.

Detect life and hazardsTapping, mmWave breathing, thermal, phone presence, gas/VOC

The node

I modelled the node as a 14-part assembly. The constraint that drives the whole geometry is that a contact sensor is worthless unless it is pressed against the structure, which means the body has to land nose-first, every time, after being thrown by a tired person onto an uneven pile.

Why the nose is a rigid stack

From the tip: flat rubber contact pad, piezo disc, solid steel strike plate. Nothing loose sits anywhere in that stack. Any compliance between the piezo and the structure shows up as lost signal, and the taps we are trying to hear are already close to the noise floor.

The solenoid lies along the long axis with its plunger pointing straight at the strike plate, so the knock energy goes into the structure rather than into the housing.

Why the steel shot sits in a ring

This is the detail I am most pleased with. The node needs mass in the nose for flight stability, and it needs to not bounce when it lands. Loose steel shot solves both, the way a dead-blow hammer does. But if you put the shot in front of the strike plate, it damps the very knock you are trying to deliver.

So the shot fills only a ring-shaped pocket around the strike plate, never ahead of it. The landing bounce dies; the knock does not.

Stability

  • Centre of mass sits at least one body diameter ahead of the centre of pressure. Solenoid, shot, magnet and battery all live in the front half.
  • Four solid TPU fins, slightly canted to add a stabilising spin.
  • A neodymium magnet ring flush with the nose rim clamps the pad down hard on steel surfaces, which is where coupling is best anyway. It is spaced from the plunger with plastic so it does not drag it.
  • If it wobbles in testing, the fix is more nose weight and bigger fins, not a redesign. That call is written into the build plan on purpose, because 2 a.m. is a bad time to start over.

The validation plan is deliberately boring: PVC-tube mockups at target weight, twenty throws, count how many land nose-first. A roughly 350 g dart with a hard nose can injure someone, so it only ever gets thrown at the pile.

Knock, blank, listen

A node cannot hear its own knock. The solenoid firing saturates its own piezo completely, so the firmware blanks that input for 150 ms after each pulse and lets the other nodes do the listening for that knock. That one constraint is why this only works as a network.

Detection runs a band-pass from 100 to 2000 Hz, takes the envelope, and counts sharp peaks above an adaptive threshold set from the rolling background. Peaks that also show up on the air microphone at similar strength are thrown out, because that is the room, not the rubble. A reply is two to four taps spaced 0.2 to 1.5 s apart inside the window.

Localisation compares peak amplitude across three or more nodes and reports a zone, not a coordinate. Claiming precision I cannot deliver would be worse than useless to someone deciding where to dig.

Getting the message out

The comms problem is the one most likely to fail in a real collapse, so the design assumes it will. Packets are about 20 bytes. Flooding with a hop limit of three and duplicate suppression by message ID. Every node keeps a priority queue, and anything it cannot send it stores.

One node runs an open access point called SOS RESCUE with a DNS server that redirects every request to a single page with three large buttons: I'm hurt, I can move, Others with me. A press becomes a top-priority packet. Phone presence elsewhere is detected by logging Wi-Fi probe requests and their signal strength, counting devices and never storing MAC addresses.

Triage is the output

The system does not report sensor values. It reports three labels, because that is what a person standing on rubble can act on.

RespondingTapped back inside the listen window
Signs of lifeBreathing on radar, or thermal blob, or a phone present
PossibleWeak or single-channel indication only
HazardVOC jump above baseline, or node over-temperature

What I expect to go wrong

  • A demo room is loud. Air-mic subtraction helps, pressing the sensors harder into the pile helps more, and calling a "quiet, listening" pause is what real teams do anyway.
  • A node lands on its side. Thermal, radar and phone channels still work; only the knocker is compromised. There is a lanyard so it can be retrieved and re-thrown.
  • The solenoid browns out the microcontroller. A boost converter trickle-charges a 2200 µF capacitor and each knock dumps that, rather than pulling the coil current through the logic supply.
  • A node breaks on a throw. Bring a spare, place it by hand, show the throw-test footage.

The cut list is written in advance and in order: spoken prompt, then handheld collector, then phone sniffing, then the second thermal camera. What never gets cut is knock-and-tap detection, the mesh, the dashboard and the triage labels, because those four things are the idea.
